### 1. The Translator Who Said It Was Hard

About eight hundred years ago, a man named Samuel Ibn Tibbon had a big job to do.

A famous book called the Guide for the Perplexed had been written in a language called Arabic. The people in Ibn Tibbon's town wanted to read it. But they didn't speak Arabic. They spoke Hebrew.

They asked Ibn Tibbon to translate it.

He said no.

He said his Arabic wasn't good enough. He said his Hebrew wasn't good enough. He said the book was too deep and too hard and he wasn't sure he understood it himself.

They asked him anyway. "Even if the translation isn't perfect," they said, "we'll learn something."

So Ibn Tibbon sat down to translate. And before he began, he wrote something. Not the translation — a letter to his readers. A letter about how hard it is to move words from one language to another.

That letter is what you're about to read.

---

### 2. Translation Isn't Just Swapping Words

You might think translating works like this: you find a word in one language, look up the same word in the other language, and swap them. Like trading cards.

It doesn't work like that.

Ibn Tibbon explained that translating is like walking on a very narrow path. There's a fence on one side and a fence on the other. On one side is Arabic. On the other side is Hebrew. If you lean too far toward Arabic, the Hebrew readers can't understand. If you lean too far toward Hebrew, you change what the Arabic actually said.

The path is so narrow that every step is a choice. And every choice changes something.

Think about it this way: if you tried to explain the word "cool" to someone who speaks a different language, what would you say? Does "cool" mean not warm? Does it mean awesome? Does it mean calm? It depends on what you meant when you said it — and the person translating has to guess.

That's what Ibn Tibbon was doing. Every word. For an entire book.

### 3. The Choices Every Translator Must Make

Ibn Tibbon noticed that Arabic and Hebrew don't line up. Here are some of the problems he found:

**Boys and girls.** In Arabic, a word might be for a boy (masculine). In Hebrew, the same idea is for a girl (feminine). Or the other way around. The translator has to pick — and either way, something feels different.

**Connecting words.** In Arabic, you might say "I resemble to something." In Hebrew, you'd say "I resemble like something." The connecting word is different. The meaning is close. But the grammar doesn't match.

**Words that don't exist.** Arabic had words for philosophy that Hebrew had never needed. Hebrew had words for praying and for following rules. But it didn't have a word for "essence" or "existence" or "purpose."

So Ibn Tibbon invented new Hebrew words. He took old Hebrew parts and put them together in new ways — like building new Lego structures from pieces you already have. The word מהות (essence). The word מציאות (existence). The word תכלית (purpose).

These words were so good that Jewish thinkers used them for hundreds of years. But they were choices. And choices leave marks.

### 4. What Gets Lost and What Gets Made

Ibn Tibbon was scared.

He wrote that even after he finished translating, he was afraid there were mistakes he hadn't noticed. He asked his readers to fix any errors they found and to be kind to him.

He wasn't just being polite. He was being honest.

When you translate, you can't see everything you've done. You're too close to the work. Some choices you made without thinking — because your brain was pulled by the language you were translating from, and you didn't even notice.

**What gets lost:** the exact feeling of the original Arabic. The way a word sounded to someone who grew up speaking that language. The cultural meaning that no translation can carry.

**What gets made:** something new. A Hebrew Guide for the Perplexed that is both Maimonides' ideas AND Ibn Tibbon's Hebrew. A book that Jewish readers studied for centuries — without realizing they were reading two voices mixed together.

Nothing arrives the same after it crosses. But that's not a bad thing. It's what translation shows us about all words.

### 5. The Translator Thinks, Not Just Copies

Here's something most people don't realize about translators:

A translator is not a copy machine. A translator is a thinker.

Ibn Tibbon understood this. He said that translating is like building a house. You need four things:
- **The builder** (that's the translator)
- **The materials** (that's the original book)
- **The shape of the house** (that's the new language)
- **The reason you built it** (that's so people can understand it)

The translator is not invisible. He is the builder. And every house he builds carries his fingerprints — his training, his mistakes, his ideas, his fears.

The translator never disappears. He is always there, in the words.

### 6. The Preface That's Really an Argument

Read Ibn Tibbon's letter again. It sounds like he's saying sorry. "I'm not good enough. Please forgive my mistakes."

But he's not saying sorry. He's making an argument.

He's saying: "This is what translation IS. This is what it costs. This is what it creates. And if you think you can do better — go ahead and try. But the path is narrow, and the choices can't be avoided."

He's not writing about translation from the outside. He's writing from inside it — while he's actually doing it. He's sitting at his desk, trying to figure out what Maimonides meant, inventing a Hebrew word that never existed before, knowing that whatever he writes will become, for hundreds of years, how Jewish people read Maimonides.

That's not an apology. That's an argument about what happens when words cross from one language to another.

---

### 7. What Translation Shows About All Words

Here's the deepest thing Ibn Tibbon's letter reveals. And it's easy to miss.

He's writing about translating between Arabic and Hebrew. But what he says is true even when two people speak the SAME language.

You and your friend might both say the word "fair." But what does "fair" mean to you? Does it mean everyone gets the same? Does it mean everyone gets what they need? Does it mean following the rules? Does it mean being kind?

You both used the same word. But you might mean different things.

Translation makes this problem visible because you have to actually cross the gap. Within one language, the gap is invisible — you assume you understand each other because you use the same words.

But the assumption is the problem. And Ibn Tibbon's letter — written eight hundred years ago about Arabic and Hebrew — is really about every conversation that has ever happened:

**The word is not the meaning. The meaning is not the word.** And the person who carries meaning — whether from one language to another, or from one person to another — is never invisible.

---

### 8. The Translator's House Is Always at Stake

Ibn Tibbon ended his letter with a request. He asked every person who reads the book to fix his mistakes and to judge him kindly.

This is not just being polite. It's something deeper.

When you translate — when you carry words from one place to another — your own house is at stake. Your understanding. Your reputation. Your self. You can't hide behind the book, because the book is yours.

And this is true for everyone who speaks, not just translators. Every sentence you say is a kind of translation — from the thought in your head to the words you choose. Every conversation is a crossing. And in every crossing, your house is at stake.

Ibn Tibbon knew this. He wrote it down. And then he began to translate.
