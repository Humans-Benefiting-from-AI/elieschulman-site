### 2. He Alone Is emet

Maimonides begins the Mishneh Torah with a foundation.

The first words of Yesodei HaTorah 1:1 are:

יְסוֹד הַיְסוֹדוֹת וְעַמּוּד הַחָכְמוֹת לֵידַע שֶׁיֵּשׁ שָׁם מָצוּי רִאשׁוֹן

*The foundation of all foundations and the pillar of wisdom is to know that there is a Primary Being.*

This is the ground. Not a proof. Not a belief. The condition for everything that follows. Maimonides does not demonstrate God's existence here. He states it the way a geometry textbook begins with axioms — the starting point, not the conclusion. The First Being exists. Everything else follows.

---

Then, in 1:1-3, he builds the structure:

**1:1:** The Primary Being brought into being all existence. Everything — heavens, earth, everything between — came into existence מֵאֲמִתַּת הִמָּצְאוֹ, "from the emes of His being."

**1:2:** If you imagine He does not exist, nothing else could exist.

**1:3:** If you imagine nothing else exists, He alone would continue to exist. All other entities require Him; He requires none of them. Therefore — לְפִיכָךְ — אֵין אֲמִתָּתוֹ כַּאֲמִתַּת אֶחָד מֵהֶם, "the emes of His being does not resemble the emes of any of their beings."

Notice what has already happened. In 1:1, emet describes the source of all existence: everything came from the emes of His being. In 1:3, emet draws the asymmetry: His emes does not resemble anyone else's. The word has appeared twice before 1:4 — and both times it is doing ontological work. Describing the quality of existence, not the accuracy of statements.

---

Then Maimonides arrives at 1:4 and makes the claim explicit.

He cites Jeremiah 10:10:

וַיהוה אֱלֹהִים אֱמֶת — *And God, your Lord, is emet.*

And he interprets it:

הוּא לְבַדּוֹ הָאֱמֶת וְאֵין לְאַחֵר אֱמֶת כַּאֲמִתָּתוֹ

*He alone is emet, and no other has emet like His emet.*

This is the sentence the entire book turns on.

---

**He alone is emet.** Not "He alone is true." Not "He alone is truthful." He alone is emet.

God does not have emet the way a person has truth — as a property that could be present or absent. God is emet. Emes is not something God possesses. It is what God is. This is identity, not attribution.

**And no other has emet like His emet.** The construction is comparative but the result is absolute. Other beings may have something called emet — Maimonides does not deny that other existents have a form of existence — but their emet is not like His emet. The word is the same. The reality is different. God's emet is non-contingent existence. A created being's emet — if the word even applies — is derivative, dependent, borrowed existence. They share the word. They do not share the essence.

---

Then Maimonides provides his own interpretive gloss through Deuteronomy 4:35:

אֵין עוֹד מִלְּבַדּוֹ — *There is nothing else aside from Him.*

And he explains:

כְּלוֹמַר אֵין שָׁם מָצוּי אֱמֶת מִלְּבַדּוֹ כְּמוֹתוֹ

*That is to say: there is no existent emet aside from Him like Him.*

This is the moment of greatest clarity — and the moment where "truth" does the most damage.

מָצוּי means "existent." Maimonides is saying: aside from God, there is no existent whose existence is emet. He is equating emet with existence. Not "there is no other true thing." Not "there is no other truthful being." There is no existent whose quality of existence is emet — genuine, self-grounded, non-contingent — aside from God. Everything else exists, but its existence is borrowed. It depends on Him. Only His existence is emet.

---

**A working definition:**

Emes is the quality of genuine existence — existence that is non-contingent, non-derivative, and self-grounded. Only God's existence has this quality. Everything else exists, but derivatively.

This is not a property of statements. It is not about accuracy. It is about the structure of reality itself — about what it means to exist genuinely versus what it means to exist dependently.

---

The word emet appears in every stage of the argument in 1:1-4. It is the thread. And at every stage, it is doing ontological work. The translation "truth" breaks the thread at every point, replacing a claim about the structure of reality with a claim about God's reliability.

---

Maimonides does not pause to draw out the implication. He states it and moves on to describe God's unity, His incorporeality, the prohibition against anthropomorphism. The implication is left for the reader.

The implication is this: if emet is genuine existence, and only God has it, then no created being has emet. Not in the sense that created beings don't exist — they do, Maimonides insists on this — but in the sense that their existence is not emet. It is real but derivative. Genuine but borrowed.

The distance between God's existence and human existence is not a difference of degree. God does not exist more; humans exist less. It is a difference of kind. God's existence is emet. Human existence is something else — real, but not emet.

If human existence is not emet, then human knowledge — which is a function of human existence — is also not emet. Human knowledge is real. It can be accurate. It can approach. But it cannot possess emet, because emet is what God is — not what humans have.

That argument is made explicit in the next chapter, in 2:10. But its foundation is already laid here. 1:4 is the seed. Everything this book argues follows from the distinction Maimonides makes in this single halakha: God alone is emet. Everything else exists, but not with emet.
