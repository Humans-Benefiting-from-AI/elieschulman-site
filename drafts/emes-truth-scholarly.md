### 2. He Alone Is emet: Underived Existence

Maimonides begins the Mishneh Torah with a foundation. The first words of Yesodei HaTorah 1:1 are:

יְסוֹד הַיְסוֹדוֹת וְעַמּוּד הַחָכְמוֹת לֵידַע שֶׁיֵּשׁ שָׁם מָצוּי רִאשׁוֹן

*The foundation of all foundations and the pillar of wisdom is to know that there is a Primary Being.*

This is the ground — not a demonstration but the condition for everything that follows. Maimonides does not prove God's existence in 1:1. He states it as the starting point, the way a geometric system begins with axioms, not with their derivation. The First Being exists. Everything else follows from that.

---

**The architecture of 1:1-3**

In 1:1-3, Maimonides constructs the argument that culminates in 1:4:

**1:1:** The Primary Being "brought into being all existence." All beings — heavens, earth, everything between — "came into existence only from the emes of His being" — מֵאֲמִתַּת הִמָּצְאוֹ. The construct form אֲמִתַּת (from אֱמֶת) appears here for the first time, describing the source of all existence.

**1:2:** If you imagine He does not exist, nothing else could exist. The dependence is unidirectional: all existence requires His; His requires nothing.

**1:3:** If you imagine nothing else exists, He alone would continue to exist. All other entities require Him; He requires none of them. Therefore — לְפִיכָךְ — אֵין אֲמִתָּתוֹ כַּאֲמִתַּת אֶחָד מֵהֶם, "the emes of His being does not resemble the emes of any of their beings."

The word emet has now appeared twice before 1:4. In both instances it performs ontological work, not epistemic work: in 1:1, it describes the quality of existence from which all being derives; in 1:3, it draws the asymmetry between divine and created existence. The English translation renders both instances as "truth" — מֵאֲמִתַּת הִמָּצְאוֹ as "from the truth of His being," and אֵין אֲמִתָּתוֹ כַּאֲמִתַּת as "the truth of His being does not resemble the truth." Both renderings collapse the ontological claim into an epistemic one, making the passage sound as though it concerns God's reliability rather than the structure of reality.

---

**The proof text: Jeremiah 10:10**

Maimonides arrives at 1:4 and makes the claim explicit. He cites Jeremiah 10:10:

וַיהוה אֱלֹהִים אֱמֶת

*And God, your Lord, is emet.*

And he interprets it:

הוּא לְבַדּוֹ הָאֱמֶת וְאֵין לְאַחֵר אֱמֶת כַּאֲמִתָּתוֹ

*He alone is emet, and no other has emet like His emet.*

This is the sentence the entire book turns on. The syntax is critical.

**הוּא לְבַדּוֹ הָאֱמֶת** — "He alone is emet." The construction is predicative: emet is predicated of God. But the predicate functions as identity, not attribution. God does not have emet the way a person has truth — as an attribute that could be present or absent, acquired or lost. God is emet. This is a statement of identity, not attribution. Emes is what God is, not what God possesses.

**וְאֵין לְאַחֵר אֱמֶת כַּאֲמִתָּתוֹ** — "and no other has emet like His emet." The construction is comparative (כְּ) but the result is absolute. Other beings may have something called emet — Maimonides does not deny that other existents have a form of existence — but their emet is not like His emet. The word is the same. The reality is different.

In the terminology of the Treatise on Logic (ch. 13), this is an amphibolous usage at best: the same word applied to God and to other beings, but the shared meaning does not constitute the essence of either. God's emet is non-contingent existence. A created being's emet — if the word even applies — is derivative, dependent, borrowed existence. They share the word. They do not share the essence.

---

**The gloss: Deuteronomy 4:35**

Maimonides does not leave Jeremiah 10:10 standing alone. He immediately provides an interpretive gloss through Deuteronomy 4:35:

אֵין עוֹד מִלְּבַדּוֹ

*There is nothing else aside from Him.*

And he explains what this means:

כְּלוֹמַר אֵין שָׁם מָצוּי אֱמֶת מִלְּבַדּוֹ כְּמוֹתוֹ

*That is to say: there is no matzui emet aside from Him like Him.*

This is the moment of greatest clarity in the passage — and the moment where the English translation inflicts the most damage.

מָצוּי means "existent," the nominal form of the root meaning "to be found, to exist." Maimonides is saying: aside from God, there is no existent whose existence is emet. He is equating emet with מָצוּי — emet with existence itself. Not "there is no other true thing." Not "there is no other truthful being." There is no existent whose quality of existence is emet — genuine, self-grounded, non-contingent — aside from God.

---

**Working definition**

Emet is the quality of genuine existence — existence that is non-contingent, non-derivative, and self-grounded. Only God's existence has this quality. Everything else exists, but derivatively; its existence depends on God's.

This is not a property of statements. It is not about accuracy or correspondence between propositions and reality. It is about the structure of reality itself — about what it means to exist genuinely versus what it means to exist dependently.

---

**The architecture of 1:1-4**

The word emet appears at every stage of the argument:

- 1:1: Everything exists from the emet of His being (source)
- 1:3: His emet does not resemble the emet of any other (asymmetry)
- 1:4: He alone is emet (identity) — and no existent has emet like His (exclusivity)

It is the thread. And at every stage, it is performing ontological work — describing the quality of existence, not the accuracy of statements. The translation "truth" breaks the thread at every point.

---

**The consequence already present in the text**

Maimonides does not pause to draw out the implication. He states it and moves on to describe God's unity (1:5-7), His incorporeality (1:8-12), and the prohibition against anthropomorphism. The implication is left for the reader.

The implication: if emet is genuine existence, and only God has it, then no created being has emet. Not in the sense that created beings don't exist — they do, Maimonides insists on this — but in the sense that their existence is not emet. It is real but derivative. Genuine but borrowed.

The distance between God's existence and human existence is not a difference of degree (God exists more; humans exist less). It is a difference of kind (God's existence is emet; human existence is something else — real, but not emet).

If human existence is not emet, then human knowledge — which is a function of human existence — is also not emet. Human knowledge is real. It can be accurate. It can approach. But it cannot possess emet, because emet is God's essence, and God's essence is what God is — not what humans have.

That argument is made explicit in 2:10, which the next chapter examines. But its foundation is already laid here. 1:4 is the seed. Everything this book argues follows from the distinction Maimonides makes in this single halakha: God alone is emet. Everything else exists, but not with emet.
