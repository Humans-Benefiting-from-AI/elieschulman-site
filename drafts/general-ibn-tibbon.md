### 1. The Translator's Confession: Ibn Tibbon Admits It's Hard

Samuel Ibn Tibbon had a problem.

Maimonides had written the Guide for the Perplexed in Judeo-Arabic. The scholars of southern France wanted it in Hebrew. They asked Ibn Tibbon to translate it — not because he was the best qualified, but because he was the only person they knew who understood any Arabic at all.

He tried to refuse. He told them his Arabic was weak. He told them his Hebrew was not good enough. He told them the book was too deep and he was not deep enough.

They asked anyway.

And so Ibn Tibbon sat down to translate one of the most difficult philosophical works ever written in any language — and before he began, he wrote a preface.

The preface is not an introduction to the book. It is not a summary. It is a confession. And it is an argument.

### 2. Translation Is Not Substitution

Most people think translation works like this: find the word in Language A, look up the equivalent in Language B, swap them. Done.

Ibn Tibbon knew it does not work like that.

He wrote about "this narrow path of translation, fenced on this side and on that." On one side: Arabic, with its philosophical depth and its grammar. On the other: Hebrew, with its biblical resonances and its rabbinic cadence. Between them: a path so narrow that every step risks falling.

The problem is not that some words don't have equivalents. The problem is that the equivalents that exist carry different weight. A word in Arabic might be technical and precise. The "same" word in Hebrew might be biblical and emotional. If you choose the Hebrew word, you bring in associations the Arabic never intended. If you keep the Arabic, the Hebrew reader cannot understand.

You cannot just swap. You must choose. And in choosing, you inscribe yourself into the text.

### 3. The Choices No Translator Can Avoid

Ibn Tibbon cataloged the specific points where Arabic and Hebrew refuse to align:

**Gender and number.** In Arabic, a word might be masculine. In Hebrew, the same concept is feminine. Or the reverse. The translator must pick — and either way, something shifts.

**Prepositions.** A word that takes one preposition in Arabic takes a different one in Hebrew. "To resemble" connects with one letter in Arabic and a different letter in Hebrew. The meaning is close. The grammar is not. The translator must decide which fidelity matters more — fidelity to the grammar or fidelity to the sense.

**Words that don't exist.** Arabic had philosophical vocabulary that Hebrew had never needed. Hebrew had words for prayer, law, and sacrifice. It did not have words for "essence," "existence," "intellectual apprehension," or "final cause."

So Ibn Tibbon invented them.

He created Hebrew words that had never existed before — built from roots and patterns that felt Hebrew enough to pass, but carried philosophical meanings Hebrew had never borne. The word מהות (essence). The word מציאות (existence). The word תכלית (final cause).

Every translator makes choices. Ibn Tibbon's choices were so successful that they shaped the entire vocabulary of Jewish philosophy for centuries. But the choices are still choices. And choices leave traces.

### 4. What Gets Lost and What Gets Created

Ibn Tibbon admitted he was afraid.

He wrote that even after finishing the translation, he feared errors would remain that he had not noticed. He asked readers to correct his mistakes and judge him favorably.

This is not false modesty. It is phenomenological honesty. The translator cannot see everything he has done, because he is too close to the work. The choices he made unconsciously — pulled by the gravity of one language while trying to serve another — are invisible to him.

What gets lost: the exact resonance of the original Arabic. The way a word felt to a reader who grew up speaking that language. The cultural weight that no translation can carry.

What gets created: a new text that is neither the original nor a transparent copy. A Hebrew Guide for the Perplexed that is simultaneously Maimonides' philosophy and Ibn Tibbon's philosophical Hebrew. A book that Jewish readers studied for centuries — not realizing they were reading a double voice.

Nothing arrives unchanged. But that is not a complaint. It is what translation reveals about all language.

### 5. The Translator Is an Interpreter, Not a Conduit

Here is what Ibn Tibbon understood that most people don't:

The translator is not a pipe. He is not a transparent channel through which meaning flows from one language to another. He is an interpreter — present in every choice, every invented word, every moment where he had to decide what the author meant before he could decide how to say it.

Ibn Tibbon framed translation using Aristotle's four causes: the translator is the agent, the text is the material, the rendered Hebrew is the form, and the reader's comprehension is the purpose. By casting translation in metaphysical terms, he elevated it from a mechanical task to a philosophical act.

But the elevation comes at a cost. If the translator is an agent and not a conduit, then the translation is not transparent. It carries the translator's fingerprints — his training, his limitations, his creativity, his anxiety.

The translator never vanishes. He is always present in the words.

### 6. The Preface That Is Really an Argument

Read it again: Ibn Tibbon's preface is not an apology. It is an argument.

He is not saying "I'm sorry this is bad." He is saying: "This is what translation is. This is what it costs. This is what it creates. And if you think you can do better, try — but know that the path is narrow, and the choices cannot be avoided."

The preface is a philosophy of translation written from inside the act of translating. It is not theory applied to practice. It is practice discovering its own theory.

Ibn Tibbon did not write about translation in the abstract. He wrote about translation while bleeding from it — while sitting at his desk, trying to render a sentence whose meaning he half-understood, inventing a Hebrew word that had never existed, knowing that whatever he wrote would become, for centuries of Jewish readers, the voice of Maimonides.

That is not a preface. That is an argument about what language does when it crosses.

---

### 7. What Translation Reveals About All Language

Here is the deepest point. And it is easy to miss.

Ibn Tibbon's preface is about translation. But what it reveals is not limited to translation.

The same gap he describes — the gap between what a word means in one language and what its "equivalent" means in another — exists within a single language. You and I may both speak English. We may both use the word "truth." But the word does not mean the same thing to both of us. It carries different weight, different history, different assumptions.

Translation makes this gap visible because it forces the crossing. Within a single language, the gap is invisible — we assume shared meaning because we share a word.

But the assumption is the problem. And Ibn Tibbon's preface, written eight hundred years ago about Arabic and Hebrew, is really about every conversation that has ever happened in any language:

The word is not the meaning. The meaning is not the word. And the person who carries meaning across — whether between languages or between two people speaking the same one — is never transparent.

---

### 8. The Translator's House Is Always at Stake

Ibn Tibbon ended his preface with a plea. He asked every reader who found an error to correct it and to judge him favorably.

This is not just courtesy. It is phenomenological truth. The translator's own house — his understanding, his reputation, his self — is always at stake in the carrying across. He cannot hide behind the text, because the text is his.

And this is true not only of translators. It is true of anyone who speaks. Every sentence you utter is a translation — from the thought you had to the words you chose. Every conversation is a crossing. And in every crossing, your house is at stake.

Ibn Tibbon knew this. He wrote it down. And then he began to translate.
