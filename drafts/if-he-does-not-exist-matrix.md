# If He Does Not Exist — Source-to-Argument Matrix

## Yesodei HaTorah 1:2

### Root passage

אִם יַעֲלֶה עַל הַדַּעַת שֶׁהוּא אֵינוֹ מָצוּי אֵין דָּבָר אַחֵר יָכוֹל לְהִמָּצְאוֹת

*If you imagine He does not exist, no other thing could exist.*

— Maimonides, Mishneh Torah, Foundations of the Torah 1:2

This is not a causative argument. It is not: God created the world, therefore the world proves God. It is phenomenological. If the knower — the one who knows any phenomena — exists, then God exists. Because the knower's existence is dependent. Remove the ground, and the knower disappears with everything else.

---

## How the root passage generates the argument

Three movements:

1. **The thought experiment.** Try to imagine God does not exist. Not as a theological proposition — as a phenomenological exercise. What happens to everything else? It vanishes. Including the one doing the imagining. The knower cannot survive the removal of the ground.

2. **The phenomenological turn.** But you are here. You are knowing. You are reading this sentence. The knower exists — not as a proof, but as a fact of experience. And if the knower exists, and the knower cannot exist without God, then God exists. Not because you reasoned from effect to cause, but because your knowing depends on a ground that, if removed, takes you with it.

3. **Dependence, not causation.** This is not "God caused the world" (a claim about the past). It is "the world cannot be without God" (a claim about the structure of existence). The difference: causation says A produced B. Dependence says B cannot be without A. Maimonides' argument in 1:2 is the second. Remove God from the picture and nothing remains — not because God destroyed it, but because nothing has the capacity to exist on its own.

## The arc

- **Chapter 1:** The thought experiment. Maimonides asks you to imagine God doesn't exist. Not as theology but as phenomenology. What happens to the world? To you? Everything vanishes — not because God is angry, but because nothing has the capacity to stand alone. The knower disappears with the known.

- **Chapter 2:** The phenomenological turn. But you are here. You are reading. You are knowing right now. The knower exists as a fact of experience. And if the knower cannot exist without God (Chapter 1), and the knower exists (you are here), then God exists. Not as a syllogism from effect to cause, but as the recognition that the ground of your knowing is the ground of everything.

- **Chapter 3:** Dependence, not causation. Maimonides does not say "God made the world" in 1:2. He says "if God is not, nothing is." The difference: making is about the past (A produced B). Dependence is about structure (B cannot be without A). This is closer to dependent origination than to the cosmological argument. The world doesn't prove God the way a watch proves a watchmaker. The world depends on God the way a shadow depends on the object casting it.

- **Chapter 4:** The chain. Everything that exists depends on something. Your knowledge depends on your mind. Your mind depends on your existence. Your existence depends on... what? The chain does not end at a first event in time. It ends at the ground of being — the one whose existence is emet, not borrowed, not derivative, not dependent on anything else. The chain ends where dependence stops. And dependence stops only at God.

The root passage returns: אִם יַעֲלֶה עַל הַדַּעַת שֶׁהוּא אֵינוֹ מָצוּי אֵין דָּבָר אַחֵר יָכוֹל לְהִמָּצְאוֹת. If you imagine He does not exist, nothing else could exist. But you exist. Therefore He exists.
