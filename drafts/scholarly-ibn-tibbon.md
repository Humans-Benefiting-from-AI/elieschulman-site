### 1. The Translator's Difficulty: Ibn Tibbon's First-Person Account

Samuel Ibn Tibbon (c. 1165–1232) opened his Hebrew translation of Maimonides' Dalālat al-Ḥāʾirīn with a preface that functions simultaneously as an apology, a methodology, and a philosophical argument. He began by refusing the pretense of mastery:

> "ויודע אני ומכיר במיעוט שלימותי בשתי הלשונות העברית והערבית, וכל שכן בלשון הערבית שידיעתי בה מעוטה."
> "I know and recognize my deficiency in both Hebrew and Arabic, all the more in Arabic, where my knowledge is meager."

This confession is not merely conventional humility. Ibn Tibbon situates his translation within the limits of his own comprehension — establishing from the outset that the translator's epistemic position is constitutive of the translated text. The phenomenology here is one of situatedness: the translator acknowledges that he cannot efface himself, and that his shortcomings will inevitably leave their trace.

The preface was written c. 1204, after the scholars of Lunel — led by R. Jonathan ha-Kohen — requested that Ibn Tibbon translate the Guide, which they had obtained from Maimonides in Egypt. Ibn Tibbon's account of this request, his reluctance, and his eventual acceptance establishes the social and intellectual conditions under which the translation was produced: not by a master of both languages, but by the only person available who understood any Arabic at all.

### 2. What the Preface Admits: Translation Is Not Substitution

Ibn Tibbon described translation as "this narrow path of translation, fenced on this side and on that" (משעול ההעתקה הצר הלזה, אשר גדר מזה וגדר מזה). The metaphor is spatial: two fences, two languages, and between them a path so narrow that deviation in either direction produces distortion.

The fences are not symmetrical. On one side lies the Arabic source — its philosophical precision, its grammatical structures, the specific ways in which Maimonides' argument depends on Arabic technical vocabulary. On the other lies Hebrew — its biblical and rabbinic resonances, its limited philosophical lexicon, the weight that every word carries from centuries of sacred use.

The translator cannot serve both. Fidelity to the Arabic requires carrying technical precision into a language that lacks the vocabulary. Fidelity to the Hebrew requires choosing words whose resonances may distort the Arabic meaning. Every step on the narrow path is a choice — and every choice inscribes the translator into the text.

This is what the preface admits: translation is not the substitution of one word for another. It is an act of interpretation so constitutive that the resulting text cannot be understood as a transparent copy of the original.

### 3. The Choices the Translator Must Make

Ibn Tibbon cataloged the specific structural mismatches between Arabic and Hebrew with the precision of a grammarian:

**Gender and number.** Arabic does not distinguish between singular feminine and plural masculine in certain constructions; Hebrew does. The translator must choose — and the choice shifts the grammatical texture of the sentence.

**Prepositional shifts.** Ibn Tibbon cited specific cases: דמה ("to resemble") takes bet in Arabic but lamed or aleph in Hebrew; קבל ("to receive") takes lamed in Arabic but min in Hebrew. These are not trivial details. They mark the points where fidelity to one language's grammar would violate the other's idiom.

**Existential constructions.** Arabic uses the word שם ("there") to express existence — "there is" — in a way Hebrew does not. Ibn Tibbon noted that Maimonides himself, when writing in Hebrew in the Mishneh Torah, was drawn into this Arabic construction (יסוד היסודות ועמוד החכמות לידע שיש שם מצוי ראשון), to the point that Hebrew readers misunderstood שם as a reference to God rather than an existential marker.

If Maimonides himself — writing in his native language, not translating — could not avoid being pulled by the gravity of Arabic constructions, how much more so the translator who is working between the two languages consciously?

### 4. What Gets Lost and What Gets Created

Ibn Tibbon's preface is explicit about what is lost: the exact resonance of the Arabic, the precision of Maimonides' philosophical vocabulary, the way a sentence feels to a reader who inhabits the source language fully.

But the preface is equally explicit about what is created. Ibn Tibbon openly admitted to neologisms:

> "בבנות בנינים שלא ימצאו בספרינו, כמו 'נתפלסף' שאבננו מ'פילוסוף' להימשך בו אחר לשון הערבי."
> "In coining forms not found in our literature, such as nithphalsef ('to philosophize'), which I built from philosophos, following the Arabic."

The translator becomes a creator. Hebrew is stretched to carry philosophical categories it had never previously borne. Words like מהות (essence), מציאות (existence), השגה (intellectual apprehension), and תכלית (final cause) were either naturalized or stabilized in philosophical Hebrew through Ibn Tibbon's choices. His work thus shaped not only the reception of Maimonides but the entire lexicon of Jewish philosophy.

What gets lost: the Arabic original's transparency to its first readers. What gets created: a Hebrew philosophical language that did not exist before Ibn Tibbon made it — and that would become, for centuries, the medium through which Jewish thinkers encountered philosophy.

### 5. The Translator as Interpreter, Not Conduit

Ibn Tibbon framed translation using Aristotelian causation:

> "וזה מפני שההעתקה היא מעשה אחד וכל מעשה יש ארבע סיבות למציאותו… והם הפועל והחומר והצורה והתכלית."
> "For translation is a single act, and every act has four causes for its existence… they are the agent, the material, the form, and the final cause."

The translator is the efficient cause (הפועל). The source text is the material (החומר). The rendered Hebrew phrasing is the form (הצורה). The reader's comprehension is the final cause (התכלית).

By casting translation in metaphysical terms, Ibn Tibbon elevated it from a mechanical process to a philosophically dignified activity. But the elevation also exposes the translator's unavoidable presence: he is not transparent; he is an agent shaping the text. The four causes do not eliminate the translator's subjectivity — they name it.

The translator is an interpreter, not a conduit. Every act of translation is an act of interpretation, and every interpretation carries the interpreter's epistemic position into the result.

### 6. The Preface as Argument, Not Apology

Read as a whole, Ibn Tibbon's preface is not an apology. It is an argument.

He is not saying: "Forgive me, this translation is poor." He is saying: "This is what translation is. This is what it costs. This is what it creates. And these costs and creations are not defects — they are the nature of the act."

The preface is a philosophy of translation written from inside the act of translating. It is not theory applied to practice. It is practice discovering its own theory — the translator, sitting at his desk, finding that the choices he must make reveal something about language that no abstract treatise could have told him.

The argument culminates in the final plea, where Ibn Tibbon asks readers to correct errors and judge him favorably. This is not closure. It is exposure. Translation is not a completed act but an ongoing one, dependent on the community of readers to repair what the translator could not see.

---

### 7. What Ibn Tibbon Reveals About All Language

The deepest implication of Ibn Tibbon's preface extends beyond translation.

The gap he describes — the gap between what a word means in one language and what its "equivalent" means in another — exists within a single language. Two speakers of Hebrew may use the same word and mean different things by it. Two speakers of English may both say "truth" and carry different realities.

Translation makes this gap visible because it forces the crossing. Within a single language, the gap is invisible: shared vocabulary creates the illusion of shared meaning. But the illusion is the problem — and Ibn Tibbon's preface, written eight hundred years ago about Arabic and Hebrew, reveals something about every conversation that has ever occurred in any language.

The word is not the meaning. The meaning is not the word. And the person who carries meaning across — whether between languages or between two people who think they share one — is never transparent.

This is the connection to Maimonides' own warning about equivocal names (Yesodei HaTorah 4:8): the same name can point to different realities, and each use must be understood from its context. Ibn Tibbon's preface is the lived experience of that principle — applied not to divine names but to the everyday work of carrying words from one language to another.

### 8. Conclusion: The Translator's House Is Always at Stake

Ibn Tibbon ended his preface with a plea:

> "ועתה, מפיל אני תחינתי לפני כל מעיין בספר הזה… שיתקננה וידינני לכף זכות."
> "Now I cast my plea before every reader of this book… that he correct [any errors] and judge me favorably."

This anxiety is phenomenological. Translation is not closure but exposure. The translator does not disappear behind the text; he becomes vulnerable — dependent on the community of readers to repair his mistakes, to see what he could not see, to carry the work forward.

The Latin root of translation (translatio) shares with transference the sense of "carrying across." Ibn Tibbon's preface exemplifies this: he carries Maimonides' words into Hebrew, but in doing so, he transfers part of himself — his philosophical training, his anxieties, his limitations, and his creativity.

The Hebrew Guide is not simply Maimonides' voice. It is Maimonides refracted through Ibn Tibbon. For centuries, Jewish readers encountered not the Judeo-Arabic original but this double voice — and through it, they encountered philosophy itself.

The phenomenology of translation insists: the translator never vanishes. He is always present in the words, carrying not only the text but also himself across. And his house — his understanding, his self, his place in the chain of transmission — is always at stake.
