The Distance Between Words and Reality

Four volumes examining what happens between apprehension and utterance, between source text and translation, between one soul and another. Each volume asks a single governing question and stops where Maimonides stops — without turning the question into an answer he did not give.

← Maimonides Library

Volume One: The Indescribable

Movement: Consciousness → the invisible seer

Governing question: What kind of knowing cannot be made an object of physical sight?

Primary corpus: Berakhot 10a; Mishneh Torah, Yesodei HaTorah 4:7–9

Stopping rule: Do not turn the consciousness inquiry into a book of negative theology.

The soul sees but is not seen. This is the paradox that opens the entire series — the one thing closest to us, consciousness itself, is the one thing we cannot find as an object among the things we observe. Maimonides names this through the analogy of Berakhot 10a and through Yesodei HaTorah 4:7–9, where forms without matter are known not by the physical eye but by what he calls the eye of the heart.

Chapters

  • The Fish and the Question — Open the paradox of consciousness as the medium closest to us and hardest to objectify.
  • Looking for the Seer in What Is Seen — State the proposed category error carefully: the attempt to find consciousness among the things consciousness observes.
  • The Soul That Sees But Cannot Be Seen — Read Berakhot 10a closely. The analogy between divine and human soul.
  • The Eye of the Heart — Read Yesodei HaTorah 4:7 closely. Forms without matter are known by a different kind of seeing.
  • Toward Soul-to-Soul Communication — Pose the relational problem that leads to Volume Four. Question, not answer.

Volume Two: In Any Language Whatsoever

Movement: Apprehension → utterance

Governing question: What is lost or altered when inwardly apprehended content becomes human speech?

Primary corpus: Treatise on Logic, chapters 10 and 14; Guide for the Perplexed, Introduction; I:1, I:50–57, I:64–65

Stopping rule: Stop before translation and before theorizing reception by another soul.

A word feels like knowledge. Naming something seems to grasp it. But Maimonides distinguishes inward apprehension from outward linguistic expression — and the Treatise on Logic, his earliest work, supplies a non-theological foundation for the gap. The word does not always reach what the mind holds. The limitation of a human utterance is not identical with the distance between words and reality, but the two are related.

Chapters

  • A Word Feels Like Knowledge — Begin with the confidence produced by naming. Not every word is merely descriptive — the Treatise distinguishes genuine definition from description.
  • Inner Speech and Outer Speech — Develop the apprehension-to-utterance distinction. What happens when thought becomes speech?

Boundary

Negative theology is not the primary proof here. The Treatise on Logic anchors this volume outside theology. Guide I:58–59 (negative attributes, silence and praise) are presumptively excluded from the spine — their immediate subject is divine unknowability, not the general inadequacy of human words.

Volume Three: Nothing Arrives Unchanged

Movement: Source text → translation

Governing question: What does fidelity require when no translation can preserve words and meaning simultaneously?

Primary corpus: Samuel ibn Tibbon's preface to the Hebrew translation of the Guide; Maimonides' letter to ibn Tibbon; ibn Tibbon's glossary (Perush ha-Millot ha-Zarot)

Stopping rule: Do not collapse the limitation of translation into the prior limitation of utterance.

Translation is an act of interpretation before it is an act of substitution. Maimonides told his translator: understand the sentence first, then render its sense clearly. Ibn Tibbon replied with a preface describing the difficulty — and a glossary showing why a translation requires a conceptual apparatus, not just a dictionary. Nothing arrives unchanged. But that is not a complaint about translation. It is what translation reveals about all language.

Chapters

  • The Translator Must Understand First — Build around Maimonides' translation rule: understand the sentence, then render its sense. Not word-for-word. Sense-for-sense.

Volume Four: The Problem of Soul-to-Soul Communication

Movement: Soul → sound → hearing → soul

Governing question: How does one invisible knower make contact with another through bodily speech?

Primary corpus: Guide for the Perplexed I:46, I:64–65; bridges from Berakhot 10a and Yesodei HaTorah 4:7–9

Stopping rule: Do not present a proposed trans-sensory answer as Maimonides' explicit doctrine.

We know no mode of communication between one soul and another other than speaking through sound and bodily organs. This is what Maimonides states in Guide I:46 — and it is the pivot of the entire series. The soul that sees but cannot be seen (Volume One) must reach another soul that also cannot be seen. The only bridge is the word — the utterance that Volume Two examined and the translation that Volume Three tested. Speech is the material bridge by which one invisible knower addresses another. Whether there is a beyond-speech answer remains an open question.

Chapters

  • The Only Means of Making Contact — Close-read Guide I:46 and formulate the sensory paradox. We know no other mode — but "we have no idea or notion" is an epistemic limitation, not a metaphysical impossibility.
